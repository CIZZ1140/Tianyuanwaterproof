### Why PU Flows Where Sheet Membranes Can't Go

A basement slab has three pipe penetrations converging at one corner. The drainage channel runs diagonally. The elevator pit has inside corners meeting at compound angles. This is not a flat surface with clean edges — this is what the construction drawings actually produce on site.

A 1-meter-wide SBS sheet membrane installer approaching this corner cuts seven or eight separate pieces. Each piece overlaps the next by 100 mm. In the corner where three laps stack on top of each other, the total sealant thickness becomes unpredictable — and one pinhole in one of those laps is a future leak path. The installation time at this single detail consumes significantly more labor than the equivalent area on an open floor.

PU does not approach this differently — it approaches this via a fundamentally different physical principle. It is a liquid. Pour it in, spread it around the pipe, and it self-levels to the lowest point. The coating fills the annular space around every penetration. It flows into the inside corners and rounds them naturally — no cutting, no fitting, no lapping. The cured film is one continuous, seamless elastomeric membrane produced by a chemical reaction, not assembled from factory-made pieces. For complex geometries, installation time can be as little as one-third of the equivalent sheet membrane detailing.

The science behind this is straightforward: isocyanate-terminated prepolymer reacts with ambient water molecules. Each water molecule generates an amine intermediate that immediately reacts with another isocyanate group, forming a urea linkage. The result is a three-dimensional crosslinked elastic network — chemically grown on the substrate, not mechanically laid onto it. At humidity levels above 70% — typical for Southeast Asian and South China construction sites — the reaction accelerates. The moisture that delays other trades is exactly what PU consumes to cure.

Type I achieves ≥550% elongation at break — suitable for substrates expected to move with thermal cycling and structural settlement. Type II delivers ≥2.45 MPa tensile strength — designed for sustained hydrostatic pressure in buried applications. Both types pass -40°C low-temperature bend without cracking, a threshold that many elastomeric products cannot maintain while preserving their room-temperature elongation values.

### Where PU Outperforms Sheet Membranes

- **Pipe penetrations and corners**: A single pass with a scraper replaces multiple cut pieces, lap seams, and edge-sealing operations. Detailing time is typically one-third or less of the equivalent sheet membrane installation at complex nodes.
- **Uneven substrates**: The liquid self-levels into surface irregularities, filling small voids and bridging minor cracks automatically. The hollow-sound defect that plagues sheet membrane installations — caused by trapped air under the membrane — does not occur with liquid-applied systems because the material is applied in direct contact, not laid down as a pre-formed sheet.
- **New-to-old concrete joints**: A layer of glass-fiber reinforcement mesh embedded between coat one and coat two bridges the joint. Differential settlement across the joint is absorbed by the elastic polymer matrix rather than concentrating stress at a rigid overlap — a failure mode that sheet membranes at construction joints are inherently vulnerable to because the membrane is continuous across the joint but not bonded to both sides in the same way.
- **Tile-ready surfaces**: Broadcast clean sand into the final wet coat before it cures. The embedded sand particles provide mechanical key for tile adhesive or cement screed — no separate bonding primer or interface treatment required. This eliminates one product, one application step, and one drying interval from the finishing sequence.
- **Leak repair**: The leaking area is chiseled open to expose the crack. PU is poured or troweled into the crack, a reinforcement patch is embedded, and the repair is sealed — confined to the breach zone. Sheet membrane repair typically requires stripping back a square meter or more around the leak to expose enough clean substrate for a proper overlap, because the existing membrane edges must be accessible for the new piece to be seamed in.

These advantages are not marketing differentiation. They are consequences of the material being a liquid at application and a solid after curing — a physical property set that factory-made sheet products, by definition, cannot match because they are produced as finished solids in fixed dimensions and must be mechanically assembled on site.
