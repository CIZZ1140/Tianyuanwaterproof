## Quick Answer: Which SBS System for Which Below-Grade Zone?

**Torch-apply Type II polyester SBS membrane on open foundation slabs and walls; use self-adhesive SBS where flame work is restricted; reserve PYG reinforcement for settlement-prone movement joints; and always install a protection layer before backfilling.** SBS modified bitumen membrane is a post-applied below-grade waterproofing system: it is bonded to the outside face of the structure (the water side) after the concrete is cast. Type II SBS stays flexible to -25°C, delivers 900+ N/50mm tensile strength with polyester reinforcement, and withstands 0.3 MPa water pressure for 30 minutes without leakage. For blind-side walls where the membrane must bond to concrete that is poured afterwards, a pre-applied system — not post-applied SBS — is the correct specification.

## Why Below-Grade SBS Systems Fail — and How the Specification Prevents It

Basement and foundation waterproofing works under conditions that roofs never see: permanent hydrostatic pressure, wet backfill, thermal contraction during winter pours, settlement cracking, and aggressive groundwater chemistry. When a below-grade bitumen membrane fails, the cause is almost always one of four specification gaps — not the material itself:

1. **Wrong material for the construction sequence.** Post-applied SBS cannot be used where the waterproofing face is inaccessible after casting (blind-side walls, contiguous pile walls) — that zone belongs to pre-applied systems.
2. **Missing protection layer.** Backfill is placed directly against the membrane, puncturing it. This is the single most common cause of below-grade leakage claims.
3. **Under-specified reinforcement.** An unreinforced or glass-fibre membrane cannot bridge the crack movement of a settlement-prone foundation; polyester (PY) or glass-reinforced polyester (PYG) carriers are required where movement is expected.
4. **No detail treatment.** Pipe penetrations, step-in wall transitions and movement joints are waterproofed with the same flat-area approach — the first points to leak.

A below-grade SBS specification that survives contact with the ground answers each of these four points zone by zone.

## The Four Zones of a Below-Grade SBS System

Water finds the path of least resistance, so a basement is best specified as four distinct waterproofing zones rather than one flat membrane area:

| Zone | Typical construction | Recommended SBS build | Why | Post-install protection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Raft / blinding slab** | Blinding concrete over soil; raft or strip footings cast on top | Torch-applied Type II PY, 4mm, single layer (or 3mm double layer for high water table) | Post-applied SBS bonds to the blinding face; polyester carrier resists puncture from rebar placement above | 50mm cement mortar screed or lean concrete before the structural raft is cast |
| **Basement walls (positive side)** | Cast concrete wall, excavation open on the outside face | Torch-applied Type II PY 4mm; **self-adhesive SBS** where flame is restricted or the wall is tight to shoring | Hot-melt bond gives a monolithic seal; self-adhesive removes hot-work risk in confined trenches | Rigid protection board or 20mm cement mortar; drainage composite with geotextile where groundwater is high |
| **Movement joints & detail zones** | Expansion joints, settlement joints, pipe penetrations, step-in wall transitions | PYG (glass-reinforced polyester) reinforcement; separate reinforcing strip at internal and external corners; sealant and collars at penetrations | PYG combines tensile strength with dimensional stability for cyclic movement; detail zones need the flexible carrier, not a flat-area sheet | Joint covers or protection boards; photographic record before backfill |
| **Planted cover slab / podium** | Structure below landscaped soil, root-penetrated planters | SBS base layer plus root-resistant top layer or root barrier, drainage composite above | Green cover slabs stay wet and are root- and chemical-exposed; the membrane must be shielded from both | Drainage board + geotextile filter over the membrane, below the soil |

The zone table is the specification's skeleton: choose the membrane build for each zone, then confirm thickness, grade and reinforcement against the project's waterproofing grade. Under common practice for habitable basements, the design calls for structural watertight concrete **plus** at least one bonded membrane layer — the SBS system is that bonded layer, not a replacement for the concrete.

## SBS, Self-Adhesive SBS or Pre-Applied: Where Each Fits Below Grade

Three bitumen-adjacent systems are frequently compared in below-grade tenders. They are not substitutes — they match different construction sequences:

| System | Installed when | Bond | Best zone | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Torch-applied SBS** | Post-cast, on the outside face | Hot-melt fusion to primed substrate | Open rafts, walls with excavation access | Requires hot-work permit; access to the wall face |
| **Self-adhesive SBS** | Post-cast, on the outside face | Factory adhesive, pressure-activated | Confined trenches, occupied buildings, flame-restricted sites | Requires clean, primed, dry substrate; lower tensile than torch-applied PY builds at equal thickness |
| **Pre-applied HDPE** | Pre-cast, self-adhesive face toward the future concrete | Chemical cross-link with curing concrete | Blind-side walls, pile caps, diaphragm walls — no access for repair | Higher material cost; application sequence must suit the pour program |

## Specifying SBS Below Grade: Grade, Reinforcement and Surface

### Performance grade: default to Type II

Underground structures rarely face the 90°C–105°C heat resistance that drives roof specifications, but they do face cold installation seasons and cold groundwater. That is why Type II — with low-temperature flexibility of -25°C — is the practical below-grade default, while Type I (-20°C) suits mild climates under a strict specification. Type II also carries the higher mechanical requirements of the standard. For a full comparison of grades, see our [SBS Membrane Type I vs Type II Guide](/blog/sbs-membrane-type-i-vs-type-ii-guide/).

### Reinforcement: PY for the flat areas, PYG for movement

Polyester (PY) reinforcement gives the membrane the elongation and tensile strength to bridge the fine crack movement of a reinforced concrete structure: Type II PY delivers 900+ N/50mm peak tensile and 40–50% elongation at peak under the product configuration. Glass-reinforced polyester (PYG) adds dimensional stability for zones where movement is concentrated — expansion joints, pile-cap transitions, and areas over soft ground. Glass-fibre-only (G) carriers are rarely the right call on a foundation that can move; reserve them for stable, low-risk flat areas if specified. The reinforcement decision is covered in depth in our [PY vs G vs PYG Reinforcement Guide](/blog/py-g-pyg-sbs-membrane-reinforcement-guide/).

### Surface: below grade, surfacing is a handling question

Below grade the membrane is covered by a protection layer, so UV resistance is irrelevant. PE film and fine sand surfaces are adequate for torch-applied and self-adhesive builds respectively — mineral granule surfacing adds weight and cost without below-grade benefit unless the project specification requires it for slip resistance during installation. What matters more than the top surface is the underside: for self-adhesive application the underside must carry the factory adhesive and release film; for torch application the underside is melted and fused, so a PE film underside is standard.

## Protection Layers: The Non-Negotiable Step

Every below-grade SBS specification should state what happens to the membrane after it is installed, before the next trade arrives:

- **Against backfill on walls:** rigid protection board (extruded or expanded polystyrene, or cement board) or a 20mm-plus cement mortar coat applied over the membrane. Where groundwater is high, a drainage composite — dimpled board with a geotextile filter — both drains the wall and protects the membrane.
- **Above the blinding slab:** a cement mortar screed or lean concrete layer protects the membrane from rebar placement and foot traffic during raft casting.
- **At the toe of walls:** the wall protection must overlap the slab screed to avoid a shear line where backfill can work its way behind the protection.
- **Geotextile:** a non-woven geotextile between membrane and coarse drainage stone prevents the stone from puncturing the membrane. Our [Geotextile Fabric](/products/geotextile-fabric-manufacturer/) range covers this filter layer.

If the backfill specification includes large, sharp aggregate directly against the wall, change the backfill spec — no membrane protection layer substitutes for clean, competent backfill.

## Installation and Acceptance Checklist for Below-Grade SBS

Installation quality decides whether the specification survives. This checklist reflects the sequence used on our own project guidance and matches the acceptance points a contractor or inspector should verify:

| Stage | Check | Pass criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | Concrete surface sound, cured, free of laitance, oil and standing water | No dust, no loose particles; surface dry or as required by the adhesive system |
| Priming | Primer applied to the full bonding face, including laps and details | Uniform coat, no puddles, cured before membrane placement |
| Placement | Membrane unrolled without folds; bonded progressively, wrinkles worked out | No bubbles, no tenting; full contact with the substrate |
| Laps | Side laps and end laps to the specified width (commonly 100mm), staggered in multi-layer builds | Lap width verified with measurements at intervals; no fish-mouths at laps |
| Details | Internal and external corners reinforced with separate strips; penetrations collared | Detail strip installed before the flat sheet; no membrane bridging a corner without reinforcement |
| Testing | Water test or vacuum-box test on completed areas, or per project method | No leakage paths detected; records kept |
| Protection | Protection layer installed before backfill or next trade | Protection continuous, including wall toe overlap |
| Records | Photographs of each zone before covering | Zone-by-zone photographic record retained for the handover file |

## SBS Data That Matters Below Grade

Values below reference the representative configuration — SBS Type II, PY reinforcement, 4mm — and the GB/T 18242 series (the current edition, GB/T 18242-2025, took effect 2026-05-01). Other grades, thicknesses, reinforcements and surfaces carry different values; confirm the applicable product TDS before specification.

| Property | Value (representative) | Why it matters below grade |
|---|---|---|
| Low-temperature flexibility (Type II) | -25°C, no crack | Winter installation and cold groundwater without embrittlement |
| Heat resistance (Type II) | 105°C, 2h, no flow or drip | Confirms the compound is properly cured (not a below-grade service condition) |
| Peak tensile strength (PY Type II) | 900+ N/50mm | Resists puncture and handles handling loads on site |
| Elongation at peak (PY Type II) | 40–50% | Bridges fine structural crack movement |
| Watertightness | 0.3 MPa, 30 min, impermeable | Hydrostatic pressure resistance under service |
| Seam peeling strength | ≥ 1.5 N/mm | Lap integrity — the most common below-grade leak point |
| Soluble content (4mm) | ≥ 2,900 g/m² | Indicates the bitumen coating weight available to form the seal |

## Procurement Checklist: What to Send When Requesting a Below-Grade Quote

B2B quotations for basement and foundation SBS are only comparable when the inquiry carries the same engineering context. Prepare these seven items before requesting pricing:

1. **Structure zones** — which zones are waterproofed: blinding slab, walls, planted cover, or all.
2. **Waterproofing grade** — the project's design grade (e.g. Grade I no-seepage requirement for habitable basements) and the specified layer build.
3. **Groundwater condition** — high water table, aggressive water chemistry, and the design head pressure.
4. **Construction sequence** — whether each zone is post-cast accessible or blind-side (this decides SBS vs pre-applied).
5. **Flame restrictions** — hot-work policy on site, which decides torch-applied vs self-adhesive.
6. **Thickness, grade and reinforcement** — the design build, or a request for the factory recommendation with rationale.
7. **Documentation requirements** — TDS, batch test reports, installation guidance and any certification needed for engineering approval.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1: Can SBS membrane be used for basement waterproofing?**

A: Yes. SBS modified bitumen membrane is an established below-grade system for basement slabs, walls and foundations, typically installed as the bonded waterproofing layer over structural watertight concrete. Type II SBS with polyester reinforcement is the common specification; self-adhesive SBS is selected for confined walls where torch work is restricted. Confirm the project's waterproofing grade and layer build before specifying.

**Q2: Torch-applied or self-adhesive SBS for basement walls?**

A: Torch-applied SBS is preferred on open basement walls where a propane flame is permitted, because the hot-melt bond fuses the membrane to the primed concrete. Self-adhesive SBS is the safer choice for confined spaces, occupied buildings, excavation shoring tight to the wall, and sites where hot-work permits are restricted. Both systems require a protection layer before backfilling.

**Q3: Do I need a protection layer over SBS membrane before backfilling?**

A: Yes — this step is non-negotiable below grade. SBS membrane is a waterproofing layer, not a puncture-resistant armour. Backfill against an unprotected membrane will damage it. Common protection includes cement mortar screed, rigid protection boards, or a drainage composite with geotextile filter, selected according to the project specification and backfill material.

**Q4: What thickness of SBS membrane is used for foundation waterproofing?**

A: 4mm is the most common single-layer specification for foundation slabs and walls, balancing puncture resistance with handling. 3mm is used in double-layer builds or low-risk zones, and 5mm where heavy backfill or deep excavation loads are expected. Confirm the specified thickness against the project design and the product TDS.

**Q5: Which is better below grade: pre-applied membrane or SBS membrane?**

A: They solve different construction sequences. Pre-applied HDPE membrane is installed before concrete is poured and bonds to the curing concrete — the right choice for blind-side walls and pile caps where the membrane cannot be inspected or repaired after the structure is cast. Post-applied SBS membrane is installed onto the finished concrete surface and is generally more cost-effective on open excavations where access allows torching or self-adhesive application.

**Q6: Which SBS grade should I specify for underground structures?**

A: Type II is the practical default below grade. Its -25°C low-temperature flexibility protects against winter installation and cold groundwater conditions, and Type II polyester-reinforced membrane delivers higher tensile strength (900+ N/50mm) for the puncture and settlement loads typical of buried structures. Type I may be acceptable in mild climates under a strict project specification.
